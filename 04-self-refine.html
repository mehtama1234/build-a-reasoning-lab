Can it fix itself?

So far we've spent more compute in ways that don't need a teacher — think longer, think many times and vote. Here's the tempting next step: let the model read its own answer, look for mistakes, and try again. It's called self-refinement. It sounds like it should obviously work. We ran it on real problems to find out.

The result

Here, it only helped

Accuracy as the model revises its own answer, round after round.

Solid line: accuracy if you take the answer from each round. It starts at 42% and after 2 rounds of self-revision it's at 58% (+17 points). Whatever else is true, every revision that changed the answer changed it for the better.
What each revision did

A revision can fix a wrong answer — or wreck a right one. Here's the tally.

The problem isn't that the model never fixes anything — it does. It's that without a way to know whether its current answer is right, it edits the good ones about as readily as the bad ones.

That last stat is the good news hiding in a small number: the model's final answer (58%) matched the best answer it found in any round (58%) — it didn't second-guess itself into a worse answer even once here. On a re-read, it either caught a slip or left a correct answer alone.
The honest part — don't overread it

It worked here, but self-refinement is fragile

Two cautions. First, this is a small sample (12 problems, 2 fixes) — a clean +17 points is encouraging, not proof, and the well-known result is that small models often can't reliably self-correct. Second, and more important: refinement only rescues errors the model can notice on a second look — here, catchable arithmetic slips. It does nothing for the errors it's confident about. Re-read a problem it fundamentally misunderstood (like the drying-shirts trap from Session 1) and it will “review” its work and re-affirm the same wrong answer. The model is still its own judge — and a judge can only catch the mistakes it's able to see.

Why this matters
  • A second look is a real, cheap gain — up to a point. When a mistake is a surface slip, re-reading catches it, and it rarely costs you a right answer. Free-ish accuracy.
  • But it's still bounded by self-judgment. The model can only fix what it can recognize as broken. Its confident, systematic errors survive every re-read.
  • The one thing that would remove that ceiling is an outside verdict on right vs wrong. And if we had that signal, we could do far more than let the model second-guess itself — we could train it.
Where we're headed

A second pass helped (42% → 58%) and cost nothing in broken answers — but it can only ever fix the mistakes the model already knows how to spot. To get past that, and to stop merely coaxing a fixed model at answer-time, we need an outside judge of right and wrong. That judge is the hinge of everything that follows — first to grade the model honestly, then to train it.
