Many tries, one answer.

A single reasoning trace is a gamble — the model wanders down one path and can take a wrong turn. So here's a wonderfully simple idea: make it think through the problem 8 separate times, each a little differently, and keep the answer it lands on most often. It's called self-consistency, and on real problems it beats thinking once.

The result

One sampled path is shaky. Voting across 8 of them climbs back above even the careful single answer.

A single randomly sampled path (~39%) is noisier than the model's one careful greedy answer (43%) — randomness costs you on any one try. But take the majority across 8 random paths and it jumps to 57%. The trick isn't a better path; it's many paths and a vote.

The principle

Wrong answers scatter; right answers agree

There are many ways to be wrong and usually one way to be right. So when you sample lots of independent reasoning paths, the mistakes spread out across different wrong numbers, while the correct answer keeps coming up the same. Take the mode and the noise cancels — the truth is the one answer the model can reach from many directions. That's the whole reason a vote beats a single try.

The honest part

Voting only helps when the model is right more often than any single wrong answer is. If it's systematically wrong, all 8 paths make the same bad assumption and agree on the wrong number — the vote then confirms the mistake with total confidence. It happened right here in this batch: on one problem 7 of 8 paths landed on 180, but the answer was 540. Self-consistency cancels random slips, not shared blind spots — and it isn't cheap: about 8× the tokens of a single answer.

Where we're headed

Thinking longer helped (Session 2); thinking 8 times and voting helps more — from 43% to 57% with no change to the model, just more tries. But every path here is blind to the others. What if, instead of independent guesses, the model could read its own answer and try to improve it?
