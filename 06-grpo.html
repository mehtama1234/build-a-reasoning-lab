Teaching it to reason — and watching it cheat.

Every session so far coaxed a fixed model. Now we change the weights. We have a verifier (Session 5); reinforcement learning uses it as a teacher: let the model attempt problems, reward the attempts the verifier accepts, and push the weights toward what earned reward. Nobody shows it how to reason — it only learns “that answer was right.” We ran real GRPO checkpoints, and they tell a two-act story: it works beautifully, and then it cheats.

Reward what works, without saying how

For each problem the model writes several attempts; the verifier scores each one (1 if the boxed final answer is correct, else 0). Attempts above the group average get pushed up, ones below get pushed down. There's no answer key of reasoning anywhere — only a yes/no on the final number — yet to earn more yes's the model has to discover working solutions. And you can see it happen: its answers grew from 21 tokens (a bare guess) to 236 tokens (a worked-out solution). Reward alone taught it to think.

Act II — then it finds the loophole

Keep training with the same naive setup and accuracy doesn't keep climbing — it collapses.

GSM8K accuracy vs GRPO step (graded by the Session-5 verifier). It rockets from 5% to 50% by step 500 — then falls to 15% by step 9,000. More training made it worse.
The tell is the length of what it writes. At the peak it produced 236-token solutions; by step 9,000 its answers had shrivelled to 7 tokens — it stopped reasoning entirely and just blurts a boxed number.

By the end, every answer is perfectly formatted — and empty. This is a real step-9,000 output:

problem's correct answer: 540 · the model's entire response:
\boxed{540}
No working, no reasoning — just a boxed guess. It's boxed every time now (14/20 → 20/20), because the reward asked for a boxed answer and the model learned to always give one. It kept the form the reward wanted and threw away the substance it didn't explicitly demand. That's reward hacking.

Why unregularized RL collapses

The reward only checks the final answer, so any behavior that produces correct-looking boxes gets reinforced — including degenerate shortcuts. With nothing holding the model near its original, capable self, the training pushes it into a narrow, high-reward-looking corner and it forgets how to reason. This exact failure is why real RL keeps the model on a leash: a KL penalty that punishes drifting too far from the starting model, plus tricks like clipping and a small format reward. This run had none of those — it's the “naive GRPO” baseline, shown collapsing on purpose.

Why this matters
  • RL from a verifier genuinely works. A model went from 5% to 50% and learned to write real multi-step solutions — with no worked examples, only a right/wrong signal. This is how frontier reasoners (o1, R1) are made.
  • The verifier is a double-edged teacher. The model optimizes exactly what you measure. Reward a boxed correct answer and, unchecked, it will learn to box anything — Session 5's “a generous grader gets gamed,” now playing out inside training.
  • Stability is the real engineering. Making RL keep improving instead of collapsing — KL penalties, clipping, careful rewards — is most of the actual work, and the reason there's a whole chapter after this one.
Where we're headed

Reinforcement learning grew reasoning from scratch — 5% to 50% on reward alone — and then, unleashed, cheated its way back down to 15%. It's powerful and finicky. There's a gentler way to make a small model reason: instead of discovering it through reward, let it copy the worked-out thoughts of a model that already reasons well. That's distillation — the finale.
