What makes a model reason?

A normal language model answers the instant you ask — one shot, no working-out. A reasoning model first talks to itself: it writes a private train of thought, then answers. It's the same network either way; the only difference is whether we let it think first. Here we take a real reasoning model (Qwen3-0.6B) and watch what that one difference does.

What “reasoning” actually is

A reasoning model generates intermediate steps — a scratchpad — before its final answer. Nothing more exotic than that.

A language model writes one token at a time, and every token it writes becomes part of what it reads next. So when it writes out its own working — “position 3 is R… position 8 is R…” — each step becomes context for the next step. It's giving itself room to work a problem instead of having to know the answer instantly. That private working is wrapped in a <think> … </think> block; what comes after it is the real answer. Turning that block on is the entire difference between the two columns above.

The honest part — reasoning isn't magic

A wrong assumption survives the reasoning

The shirts question is a trick: shirts dry in parallel, so 20 shirts still take 4 hours, not 16. The model thinks about it carefully — “5 shirts take 4 hours, so one shirt takes 0.8 hours, so 20 shirts take 16” — reasoning fluently the whole way, and confidently reaches the wrong answer. Step-by-step thinking makes each step follow from the last; it does not check whether the very first assumption was right. Reasoning buys you worked arithmetic, not wisdom — a theme we'll keep testing.

Why this matters
  • Reasoning is a runtime choice, not a different model. The same weights either answer instantly or think first — turning on a scratchpad is all it takes.
  • Thinking converts hard one-shot problems into easy step-by-step ones. Counting letters is impossible to eyeball but trivial to do one position at a time.
  • It's a compute-for-accuracy trade, and it has limits. More thinking costs more tokens, and it can't rescue a wrong assumption. The rest of the lab is about spending that compute well.
Where we're headed

A reasoning model is an ordinary language model given permission to think out loud before it answers — and on the right problem, that scratchpad is the difference between “1” and “3.” Everything from here is about making the thinking better: measuring how much it helps, thinking several times and voting, letting the model fix itself, and finally training a model to reason well with reinforcement learning.
