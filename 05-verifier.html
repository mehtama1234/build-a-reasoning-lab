How do you grade a thought?

Last session hit a wall: to get better, the model needs an outside verdict on right vs wrong. So we have to build that verdict — a verifier. It sounds trivial: check if the answer is correct. But the model's “answer” is a page of reasoning, and how you pull a verdict out of it changes the score enormously. Here we grade the same 25 real answers four different ways and watch the number swing.

Exact-match says the model is almost never right — because it never writes only the number. “The number appears somewhere” says it's right far more often than it deserves — because gold numbers turn up mid-calculation even when the final answer is wrong. The truth is in between, and you only get it by pulling out the final answer and comparing it properly.

Only some of the traces end in a tidy “the answer is X.” The rest trail off, restate, or never commit — so the verifier needs a fallback, and a well-behaved model should be rewarded for stating its answer clearly. Hold that thought: it becomes a training signal next session.
Why this matters

The verifier is the reward — get it wrong and the model games it

Everything after this trains the model against this verdict. So it has to be strict (never credit a wrong answer — or the model learns to sprinkle the right-looking number into its reasoning and collect the reward without solving anything) and robust (never blame a right answer for bad formatting — or the model is punished for being correct). For math there's a clean way to get both: pull the final stated answer, normalize it, and check it against a known gold answer. That's the signal the rest of the lab runs on.

  • Evaluation is not an afterthought — it's a design decision. The same model scored 0% or 64% or 44% depending only on the grader.
  • A generous grader is worse than a strict one. False credit is the thing a trained model will learn to exploit.
  • Math is the sweet spot for reasoning research precisely because a correct final answer is checkable — you have a gold number to compare against.
Where we're headed

We now have the missing piece from Session 4: a trustworthy judge of right and wrong — extract the final answer, normalize it, compare to gold. With that verdict in hand, we don't have to settle for picking better answers at runtime. We can push the verdict backward into the weights — reward the model for answers the verifier accepts, and let it learn to reason better. That's reinforcement learning, and it's next.
