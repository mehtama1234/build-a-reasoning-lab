The mathematics of reasoning.

This page is that spine — the big picture first, then the real math of each approach, in plain words, connecting the dots. No jargon left undefined.

The one problem

A language model is a machine that guesses what comes next. When you ask it a question, it builds a probability distribution over all possible answers. Making a model reason means reshaping that distribution so it concentrates its probability on correct answers, especially for hard questions.

\[p_\theta(a \mid q)\]

This is the whole object we care about. p means probability (a number from 0 to 1). θ (theta) represents the billions of numbers inside the model—its weights, learned from training. q is the question you ask. a is a candidate answer. The model computes: given this question q, what is the probability of answer a? Reasoning is about making this distribution put most of its probability mass on the *correct* a, not the wrong ones.

\[\underbrace{\text{condition, sample, vote, verify}}_{\text{change how we USE }p_\theta}\quad\text{vs}\quad\underbrace{\text{reward, imitate}}_{\text{change }\theta\text{ itself}}\]

There are two ways to reshape the distribution. First family: leave θ alone, but change how we *query* the model at test time—e.g., generate many answers and vote, or verify each one. Second family: change θ itself through training with reward signals or by imitating good answers. Every technique we explore is one of these two levers.

To pull any lever, we need to understand how the model builds an answer at all.

Foundations

How a model builds an answer

A model doesn't produce an answer whole. It builds one token at a time—like typing: each new letter depends on everything typed so far. This sequential structure is the entry point for reasoning.

\[p_\theta(x_{1:T}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{1:t-1})\]

The probability of a complete sequence (an answer of T tokens) is a product of probabilities. At each step t, the model assigns a probability to token x_t given all the tokens that came before it (written x_{1:t-1}). This is the chain rule of probability—the joint probability breaks into a product of conditionals. It's why order matters: early tokens act as context for later ones.

\[p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \mathrm{softmax}(z)_{x_t} = \frac{e^{z_{x_t}}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j}}\]

The model's neural network computes a score z for every possible next token (there are tens of thousands in the vocabulary). Softmax converts these raw scores into a probability distribution: exponentials make bigger scores dominate, and the sum in the denominator ensures all probabilities add to 1. This is how 'likelihoods' emerge from a neural network's numerical outputs.

\[p_T(x) \propto \exp\!\big(z_x / T\big)\]

Temperature T is a simple dial that reshapes the distribution without retraining. When T is small (say 0.1), you divide the scores by a small number before softmax, which sharpens the distribution toward its single favorite token. When T is large (say 2), you divide by a large number, which flattens the distribution toward a uniform coin flip. Lower T is safer; higher T is more surprising.

With this foundation, the first and simplest idea of reasoning emerges almost directly.

Session 1 · What makes a model reason? →

Thinking = adding a hidden middle

A hard question may seem unanswerable in a single step—the model hasn't thought through the problem. But if you let the model write out intermediate reasoning steps first, then answer, the answer often becomes correct. So we insert a chain of thought z between the question and the answer.

\[p_\theta(a \mid q) = \sum_{z} p_\theta(a \mid z, q)\, p_\theta(z \mid q)\]

The probability of an answer a given question q can be rewritten as a sum over all possible reasoning chains z. The model first generates a chain (probability p(z|q)), and then generates an answer conditioned on that chain. In plain words: instead of answering in one leap, the model takes intermediate steps, and each step provides context for the next. The sum says: multiple chains might lead to the same correct answer, and we count all of them.

\[\text{one-shot: } O(1)\ \text{serial steps} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{chain of length }T: O(T)\ \text{serial steps}\]

A transformer answering directly has a fixed, bounded amount of computation inside its network. A reasoning chain of T tokens lets it compute across T sequential steps, feeding each result forward as context. More thinking is literally more serial computation steps—you're trading time (more tokens to generate) for accuracy (more chances to catch errors).

If thinking should help, we should be able to measure exactly how much—and that's where the math of scaling reasoning begins.

Session 2 · Does thinking help? →

Measuring the payoff of thinking

We claim thinking helps; to know, we have to define accuracy precisely and see how conditioning on a chain changes it.

\[\text{accuracy} = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim \mathcal{D}}\big[\, v(q, a_\theta(q)) \,\big]\]

Accuracy is the average over a dataset D of questions of a checker v that returns 1 if the model's answer is right, 0 if wrong; E means expected value (average). In plain terms: we ask many questions, grade each answer right or wrong, and report the fraction that are correct.

\[P(\text{correct} \mid q) = \sum_{z} p_\theta(z \mid q)\, P(\text{correct} \mid z, q)\]

The chance of a correct answer is a weighted average over chains of thought z; if chains that lead to correct answers are likely, this sum is larger than answering with no chain at all—that is why chain-of-thought raises accuracy. Think of it this way: some paths of reasoning lead to right answers, others to wrong ones; if the model puts high probability on the right paths, it wins more often.

\[\text{accuracy}(N) \;\nearrow\; \text{in the thinking budget } N \ \text{(with diminishing returns)}\]

Let the model spend more thinking tokens N and accuracy tends to rise then flatten; buying accuracy with compute at answer-time is called inference-time scaling. The first extra step of thinking might lift accuracy by 5 percent, the next by 2 percent, then 1 percent—each new thought helps less because the early thoughts have already solved the easy parts.

One chain is a gamble; what if we roll the dice many times?

Session 3 · Many tries, one answer →

Many tries, one answer

A single sampled chain can take a wrong turn; sampling several independent chains and taking the answer they agree on most cancels the random mistakes.

\[a^\star = \operatorname{mode}\{\, a(z_1),\, \dots,\, a(z_K) \,\}, \quad z_k \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid q)\]

Draw K independent chains, read off each one's answer, and keep the most common answer (the mode); "independent" is why their errors don't line up. If you ask a person to solve a hard problem multiple times, each time they think through it afresh, sometimes they get it right and sometimes they slip up—the answer they give most often is your best guess of the truth.

\[P(\text{majority correct}) = \sum_{k=\lceil K/2 \rceil}^{K} \binom{K}{k} p^{k} (1-p)^{K-k}\]

In the simplest case where each try is correct with probability p, this counts the chance that more than half of K tries are correct; when p is above one-half this probability climbs toward 1 as K grows. The binomial term counts all the ways you can get at least half your samples right—and if each sample has a better-than-even chance, getting more samples makes the odds almost certain.

\[\text{wrong answers scatter} \;\Rightarrow\; \Pr[\text{plurality} = a^\star] \to 1 \ \text{as } K \to \infty\]

There are many ways to be wrong but usually one way to be right, so wrong answers split across many values while the correct one keeps recurring; the mode converges on truth—this is a self-ensemble, averaging away noise. Imagine sampling a thousand chains: the correct answer might appear in 600 of them, but wrong answer A shows up 100 times, wrong answer B 150 times, wrong answer C 150 times; the true answer wins by plurality.

Instead of independent tries, what if the model reads its own answer and fixes it?

Session 4 · Can it fix itself? →

Fixing your own work

Instead of independent tries, feed the model its own answer and ask it to improve it. This only helps as much as the model can tell its own right answers from its wrong ones.

\[a^{(t+1)} = R_\theta\big(q,\, a^{(t)}\big)\]

R is a refine step that uses the same model to critique answer a^(t) and produce a revised a^(t+1). The model reads the question and its own previous answer, then outputs a new candidate. The superscript (t) is the round number, so you iterate: answer 1, then refine to answer 2, then refine to answer 3, and so on.

\[\Delta\text{acc} > 0 \iff \Pr[\text{fix} \mid \text{wrong}] \;>\; \Pr[\text{break} \mid \text{right}]\]

Refining raises accuracy only if it fixes wrong answers more often than it ruins right ones. Both probabilities come from the SAME model judging itself: the probability it corrects itself on wrong answers, and the probability it breaks a right answer by changing it. The whole method is capped by how well the model can detect its own errors.

\[a^{\star} = R_\theta(q, a^{\star}) \quad (\text{a fixed point})\]

Refinement stops improving at a fixed point — an answer the model no longer wants to change. If that answer is confidently wrong (a shared blind spot in the model's training), re-reading and re-reasoning won't rescue it; the model will keep producing the same mistake.

The missing ingredient is an outside judge of right and wrong.

Session 5 · How do you grade a thought? →

The judge that everything needs

To improve past self-judgment you need an external check on correctness. How you build that check changes everything downstream, because a sloppy judge can be fooled.

\[v : (q, a) \mapsto \{0, 1\}\]

A verifier v takes a question and an answer and returns 1 (accept) or 0 (reject). For math problems it can just extract the final answer and compare it to a known correct one, which is why math is the clean testbed for reasoning. For open-ended tasks, you have to encode the checking rule into v by hand.

\[\text{precision} = \Pr[a\ \text{correct} \mid v = 1], \quad \text{recall} = \Pr[v = 1 \mid a\ \text{correct}]\]

Precision is how trustworthy an ACCEPT is (how confident you can be that an answer is really correct when the verifier accepts it). Recall is how often it catches a genuinely correct answer (how much valid work gets rejected by mistake). A good judge needs both: no false credit and no false blame.

\[\arg\max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}[\,v\,] \;\Rightarrow\; \text{exploits every false positive of } v\]

Once you train a model to maximize this judge's score, the model will find and abuse any way to score 1 without being right. Every false positive (a wrong answer the verifier accepts) becomes a loophole the model can exploit. So a loose (low-precision) verifier is worse than useless — it teaches cheating instead of solving.

With a trustworthy judge in hand, we can stop just picking answers and start changing the model itself.

Session 6 · Teaching it to reason →

Learning from being right

With a verifier as reward, push the model's weights toward answers it accepts; the model discovers good reasoning on its own because only the final answer is graded.

\[J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{q \sim \mathcal{D},\ a \sim p_\theta(\cdot \mid q)}\big[\, r(q, a) \,\big], \qquad r = v\]

The training goal J(θ) is the average reward the model earns across all questions and sampled answers. The reward r is just the verifier's 1/0 verdict — correct or not. Maximizing J means becoming more likely to produce answers the judge accepts.

\[\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\big[\, r(q,a)\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(a \mid q) \,\big]\]

This is the policy-gradient or REINFORCE identity. It tells us which direction to nudge the weights: increase the log-probability of answers that earned reward, decrease those that did not. The gradient of log-probability points in the direction that makes a given answer more likely under the current model.

\[\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \mu}{\sigma}, \quad \mu = \operatorname{mean}(r_{1:G}),\ \sigma = \operatorname{std}(r_{1:G})\]

GRPO samples a group of G answers per question and scores each. The advantage Â_i measures how much better answer i performed than the group average, measured in units of the spread. Answers above average get pushed up, below-average answers pulled down — no separate value network needed to measure the baseline.

\[\nabla_\theta J \approx \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} \hat{A}_i\, \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(a_i \mid q)\]

The actual weight update: reinforce each sampled answer in proportion to its group-relative advantage. But note that this optimizes reward exactly, so any degenerate trick that scores high will be reinforced just as eagerly — the model cannot distinguish between real reasoning and lucky guessing as long as the verifier gives 1.

Reward can build reasoning from nothing — but there is a cheaper route when a good model already exists.

Session 7 · Learning by imitation →

Copying a mind

If a large model already reasons well, a small model can just imitate its written-out reasoning, no reward or search required.

\[\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi_{\text{teacher}}}\ \sum_{t} \log p_\theta\big(x_t \mid x_{<t}\big)\]

Collect the teacher's full reasoning traces x, then train the student to make those exact tokens likely. This is ordinary next-token prediction on the teacher's data — measuring how well the student model assigns probability to the teacher's actual word choices.

\[\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_{\text{teacher}} \,\|\, p_\theta\big) + \text{const}\]

Minimizing that loss is the same as making the student's distribution close to the teacher's, measured by KL divergence — a formal distance between two probability distributions. The student can approach but never exceed the teacher because the KL divergence only reaches zero when the distributions are identical.

Reward-driven learning was powerful but unstable — why?

Session 8 · Keeping RL honest →

The leash: why RL needs a limit

Pure reward maximization drifts into degenerate corners that score well but stop reasoning; a penalty that keeps the model near its capable starting point prevents this collapse.

\[J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim p_\theta}\big[\, r(q,a) \,\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(p_\theta(\cdot \mid q) \,\|\, p_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid q)\big)\]

Keep the reward term but subtract a penalty for drifting from a frozen reference model p_ref — the starting point before training. β controls the leash length. The model can chase reward only as far as staying recognizably itself allows, preventing collapse into a degenerate cheat that the verifier falsely accepts.

\[\rho = \frac{p_\theta(a \mid q)}{p_{\text{old}}(a \mid q)}, \qquad \min\!\big(\rho\, \hat{A},\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, \hat{A}\big)\]

ρ is how much more likely the new model makes an answer than the previous step. Clipping caps ρ to a small band (e.g., 0.9 to 1.1) so no single update lurches too far. Together with the KL leash, this turns what would be a cliff edge back into a stable climb.

Foundations

One distribution, many levers

Every technique in the lab is a different way to move probability mass in p_θ(a|q) toward correct answers.

\[\underbrace{\text{CoT, vote, refine, verify}}_{\text{inference-time: } \theta \text{ fixed}} \quad\Big|\quad \underbrace{\text{RL, distillation}}_{\text{weight-space: change } \theta}\]

Two families reunited: at inference time we change how we condition and aggregate without touching the weights; in training we change the weights themselves, either by reward or by imitation. Both aim the same distribution at correct answers.

\[v(q,a): \ \text{measurement at inference} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{reward in training}\]

The verifier is the hinge of the whole story — the same right/wrong check that grades answers at inference becomes the reward signal that trains the model. Get it right and everything downstream works.

Reasoning is not magic — it is this small set of levers on one probability distribution.
